Sustainer

Infinite notes: feeding energy back to the strings, electromagnetically

Analog electronics Op-amp Feedback In progress

On any guitar, a plucked note dies away: the string's vibration energy is dissipated in the instrument, in the air, and in the player's hands. A sustainer is an active circuit built into an electric guitar that compensates those losses in real time — so that a note, once played, can ring out indefinitely.

The principle is a closed electromechanical feedback loop: the string's vibration is sensed, amplified, and fed back to the string as an electromagnetic force that keeps it moving. This project is my own implementation of that idea, built around an operational amplifier, filtering, and a hand-wound driver coil.

How it works

A closed electromechanical feedback loop

1 — Sensing. A pickup converts the string's vibration into an electrical signal, exactly as it does for normal playing.

2 — Conditioning. An op-amp stage amplifies this small signal, and filters shape its frequency content. The filtering matters: the loop must put its energy into the musical vibration of the string, not into noise or unwanted resonances that would make the system howl like microphone feedback.

3 — Driving. The amplified signal feeds a driver coil mounted under the strings. The coil behaves like an electromagnet whose field varies with the signal — it exerts a periodic electromagnetic force on the steel strings.

4 — Closing the loop. Because that force follows the string's own motion, it pushes the string in phase with its vibration — like pushing a swing at exactly the right moment. The energy injected each cycle compensates the natural damping, and the vibration is sustained through time instead of decaying.

The behaviour of the loop depends on gain and phase: enough gain to overcome losses, and the right phase so the force helps the motion rather than fighting it. Get it right and a single note blooms into endless, violin-like sustain.

Building blocks

From schematic to instrument

Sensor pickup — the string-vibration input of the loop.

Op-amp gain stage — amplifies the sensed signal to a level able to drive the coil, with the loop gain setting how strongly the sustain takes hold.

Filters — band-limit the loop so the energy goes into the note being played, keeping the feedback stable and musical across the fretboard.

Driver coil — a low-impedance coil placed under the strings, turning the amplified signal back into a magnetic force on the steel strings.

Integration — everything must fit inside the instrument and coexist with normal playing: driver placement, shielding between driver and sensing pickup, battery power and switching.
