SG Custom

A matter of aesthetics — turning a modern replica into a 1961 relic

Lutherie Finishing Artificial ageing

Largely inspired by Angus Young when choosing my first instrument, I started out on a replica of a Gibson SG. The instantly recognisable SG shape ("Solid Guitar") came from Ted McCarty, who designed the model in 1961. The instrument's "horns" guarantee playing comfort along the whole neck, while the body bevels lighten the guitar and make it more striking.

The replica I owned, however, was slightly different in its construction: the bevels of the horns and body were less pronounced, and the headstock shape differed from Gibson's. Beyond the contours, the brand-new look of the instrument felt too "perfect" for a guitar that ought to be vintage.

So I decided to re-carve the contours and artificially age the instrument, to get as close as possible to a genuine 1961 Gibson SG.

Step 1

Re-carving the contours

The first challenge was stripping off the thick layer of paint and polyurethane varnish to bring the wood back to bare grain.

Once the grain was exposed, I could start deepening the bevels with wood rasps, working them towards the profile of the 1961 originals.

It was also the opportunity to enlarge the pickup cavities, which now lets me disconnect or replace each pickup independently.

With the contours redrawn, the body was repainted with a primer coat, three coats of paint and a coat of varnish.

Visually the guitar was now much more faithful to the 1961 model — but one detail was missing. Through use, storage and the climates they were exposed to, those 1960s guitars have aged. Even the best-preserved show the same phenomena on the finish, the hardware and the plastics.

Step 2

Artificial ageing

To replicate the scars of time I studied several instruments found online, but my main inspiration came from the book « Guitare, un art de vivre » — Louis Bertignac's SG.

I started by damaging the varnish and paint of the body to simulate heavy wear. The plating and hardware in contact with the hand was dulled to reproduce the action of sweat on gold parts. The volume and tone knobs were swapped for a format historically correct for the era.

All that remained was reproducing the crazing of the nitrocellulose finish found on every vintage guitar — again using a 1961 SG Standard as the reference.

I recreated the random crack patterns across the whole body and headstock, making them converge towards the most stressed points: the output jack, the potentiometers and the screws.

I also drew on the work of luthier Michaël Boudoux (Relic'Art Guitars) for the ageing of this instrument.

As a first experience in lutherie, electronics and finishing, this project was my training ground for the many crafts an electric guitar calls upon. Beyond the look I was after, my greatest satisfaction is being able to fully modify, customise and even maintain my own instrument — entirely on my own.
